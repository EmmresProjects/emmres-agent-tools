# The Salt Road

## Section 1 - The Tithe

Maren had been very carefully counting the sacks since dawn, and she was quite certain that the tally was short by two. The barn smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool. Outside, the rain ran down the salt road in long grey ropes, and the bell in the upper town began to ring.

She felt that something was wrong before she could see it. She noticed Dov standing in the doorway with his hat in his hands, and she realized he had been standing there for some time. He seemed to be waiting for her to speak first.

"The clerk came down at four," he said. "He said the tithe was short. He said it in front of everyone."

Maren's heart pounded. It was the calm before the storm, everyone knew that, and she had been very, very good at keeping her face still for eleven years. She said nothing. Dov waited. The rain went on and on, and somewhere behind the barn a dog was barking at nothing at all.

"It was not short when I counted it," she said at last. "It was short when he counted it."

"That is a serious thing to say."

"It is a true thing to say."

Dov looked at the sacks, and then at the door, and then at the sacks again. He was a fair man, everyone said so, and being fair had cost him nothing in his whole life. Maren could feel her hands shaking, and she put them in her pockets so that he would not see.

## Section 2 - The Clerk's Ledger

By noon the rain had stopped and the upper town was full of people who had decided to be somewhere else. Maren was walking quickly. She was thinking about the ledger and about the way the clerk had held it against his chest, the way a man holds a thing he has already decided to lie about.

The office was cold and clean and smelled of floor polish. The clerk was writing with a very small pen. He did not look up.

"I want the tally," Maren said.

"You have had the tally."

"I want the page."

The clerk set down the pen, quite deliberately, and it seemed to her that he had rehearsed this. He told her that the page was not a public page. He told her that the tithe was a matter for the upper town now. He told her that she was, of course, free to make a complaint, and that complaints were heard in the spring.

Maren went out into the street. The river was low and the light was grey and the whole town looked like a thing that had been left out in the weather. She thought about her mother, who had counted salt for forty years and never once been short, and she thought about the way the clerk had said the word "complaint", as though it were a kind of weather.

## Section 3 - What the Neighbours Knew

That evening three of the neighbours came to the barn, which was more than Maren had expected. They stood in a half circle and looked at the sacks and did not say very much.

"It is the same for all of us," said one of them, a woman whose name Maren had never learned properly. "It is the same every year, and every year we say it is the same, and then it is spring."

Nobody disagreed. The lamp hissed. Somewhere the dog started up again, and then stopped.

Maren looked at the two missing sacks, or at the place where they would have been, and she understood that nothing was going to happen. It was the tip of the iceberg; everyone knew it and no one would say it. The neighbours went home one by one. Dov did not come. Maren sat with the door open until the cold made her get up, and then she went to bed, and in the morning the bell rang again, very exactly, at the same hour, and she got up and began to count.
